# Embodied Non-Harm, Retreat Mind, and the Tick Story

## Content

Dhamma Talk Outline — Embodied Non‑Harm, Retreat Mind, and the Tick Story
I. Opening (1–2 minutes)
• “Last time we talked about metta and the structure of retreat — the schedule, the rhythm, the container.”
• “Tonight I want to go a layer deeper: what actually happens inside when the mind has enough space for the dhammas to show themselves.”
• “This is about embodied non‑harm — not as a rule, but as something that arises naturally when the mind is quiet enough.”
---
II. Spirit Rock as Ethical Field (2–3 minutes)
• “Spirit Rock has its own ethics toward the land — toward the animals, the creatures, the quiet.”
• “The deer, the turkeys, the quail, the lizards… they behave differently there. Humans aren’t broadcasting threat.”
• “Retreat creates conditions where non‑harm isn’t an idea — it’s the atmosphere.”
• “When the mind settles into that atmosphere, it starts to see intention in very small moments.”
---
III. The Space for Dhammanupassanā (2–3 minutes)
• “On retreat, the mind has time. Time to slow down. Time to notice. Time to see the dhammas as they arise.”
• “Noticing aversion, noticing care, noticing uncertainty, noticing the lean toward the future — all of that becomes visible.”
• “This is dhammanupassanā in its natural habitat: not on the cushion, but in the ordinary movements of the day.”
---
IV. The Tick Story (5–6 minutes)
Set the scene:
• “I was walking the path near the bench labeled anicca — the one that overlooks the valley.”
• “I saw a tall stalk of grass with a tick clinging to it.”
The mind reveals itself:
• “First, a little aversion.”
• “Then care — I didn’t want someone brushing against it.”
• “Then uncertainty — I didn’t know what the right thing was.”
• “Then intention — non‑harm, as best as I could manage.”
• “I lifted the stalk gently and laid it down off the path.”
The humor:
• “And of course, I thought: that tick was probably my mother once.”
• “Humor can soften the mind around the truth of conditionality.”
The point:
• “It wasn’t a big moment. But it was a clear one. A moment where non‑harm wasn’t a rule — it was embodied.”
---
V. The Sutta — MN 136 (2–3 minutes)
Just enough for a fellow teacher to recognize it:
• “There’s a sutta — MN 136, the Mahākammavibhaṅga — where the Buddha warns against collapsing a single moment into a tidy story.”
• “He gives four cases:
	◦ bad deed → bad result
	◦ bad deed → good result
	◦ good deed → good result
	◦ good deed → bad result”
• “The point is: we simply don’t know the full karmic arc of any action.”
• “What we do know is our intention.”
• “That’s the only part we actually control.”
Tie it back:
• “That’s exactly what happened with the tick. I didn’t know the outcome. I didn’t pretend to know. I acted from non‑harm.”
---
VI. Ajahn Chah and “mai nae” (2 minutes)
• “Ajahn Chah used a phrase constantly: mai nae — ‘not sure,’ ‘uncertain,’ ‘not a sure thing.’”
• “He even said anicca can be understood as ‘uncertain.’”
• “Retreat teaches us to live inside mai nae — to act cleanly without needing certainty.”
---
VII. Bringing It Home (2–3 minutes)
• “Retreat gives us unusual conditions — quiet, simplicity, fewer inputs — so we can see the dhammas in small acts.”
• “The question is whether we can carry that clarity back into daily life, where everything pushes us toward quick conclusions.”
• “Can we act from non‑harm even when we don’t know the outcome?”
• “Can we stay with ‘not sure’ without collapsing into a story?”
---
VIII. Closing (1 minute)
• “If all you take from this talk is: try to be nicer to bugs, we’re good.”
• “But if you also take with you the sense that non‑harm can be embodied, and that uncertainty is not a problem but a teacher — that’s even better.”
• “May your practice meet you in the small moments.”

MN 136 – The Longer Analysis of Deeds (Mahākammavibhaṅga Sutta) The wanderer Potaliputta visits the junior monk Samiddhi, claiming the Buddha taught that bodily and verbal deeds are futile and only mental deeds are real. Samiddhi rejects this, but when asked what feeling follows an intentional deed of body, speech, or mind, he answers simply "suffering." Potaliputta leaves without responding; Samiddhi reports to Ānanda, and they go to the Buddha. The Buddha criticizes Samiddhi's categorical answer: Potaliputta was asking about the three feelings, and the correct reply is that pleasant, painful, and neutral deeds each produce their corresponding feeling. He then gives his "great analysis of deeds." There are four possible combinations: bad conduct with rebirth in hell; bad conduct with rebirth in heaven; good conduct with rebirth in heaven; and good conduct with rebirth in hell. He explains how ascetics who develop the divine eye observe a single case and wrongly generalize — "all who do bad deeds go to hell," "there is no such thing as bad deeds," etc. — each insisting their view alone is true. The Buddha grants the individual observations but rejects the universal claims: results depend on which deed ripens, including other deeds done previously or later, or the view undertaken at death, and deeds may ripen in this life, the next, or beyond. Hence there are deeds that appear effective but aren't, and vice versa — apparent karmic outcomes cannot be taken as proof of simple one-to-one causation.

# The Longer Analysis of Deeds 

So I have heard. At one time the Buddha was staying near Rājagaha in the Bamboo Grove, the squirrels’ feeding ground. 

Now at that time Venerable Samiddhi was staying in a wilderness hut. Then as the wanderer Potaliputta was going for a walk he came up to Venerable Samiddhi and exchanged greetings with him. This is the only time we meet Potaliputta. His name suggests he hails from the capital of Assaka, variously spelled Potali or Potana ([DN 19:36.4](https://suttacentral.net/dn19/en/sujato#36.4)). | As for Samiddhi, see note on [MN 133:1.3](https://suttacentral.net/mn133/en/sujato#1.3). When the greetings and polite conversation were over, he sat down to one side and said to him: 

“Reverend Samiddhi, I have heard and learned this in the presence of the ascetic Gotama: ‘Deeds by way of body and speech are done in vain. Only mental deeds are real.’ And: ‘There is such an attainment where the one who enters it does not feel anything at all.’” 

“Don’t say that, Reverend Potaliputta, don’t say that! Don’t misrepresent the Buddha, for misrepresentation of the Buddha is not good. And the Buddha would not say this: ‘Deeds by way of body and speech are done in vain. Only mental deeds are real.’ There is indeed no such statement in the suttas. But, reverend, there is such an attainment where the one who enters it does not feel anything at all.” This is the cessation of perception and feelings. 

“Reverend Samiddhi, how long has it been since you went forth?” 

“Not long, reverend: three years.” 

“Well now, what are we to say to the senior mendicants, when even such a junior mendicant imagines their Teacher needs defending? After doing an intentional deed by way of body, speech, or mind, reverend, what does one feel?” 

“After doing an intentional deed by way of body, speech, or mind, reverend, one feels suffering.” It is this answer of Samiddhi’s that is criticized below. Then, neither approving nor rejecting Samiddhi’s statement, Potaliputta rose from his seat and left. 

Soon after he had left, Venerable Samiddhi went to Venerable Ānanda, and exchanged greetings with him. When the greetings and polite conversation were over, he sat down to one side, and informed Ānanda of all they had discussed. 

When he had spoken, Ānanda said to him, “Reverend Samiddhi, we should see the Buddha about this matter. Come, let’s go to the Buddha and inform him about this. As he answers, so we’ll remember it.” 

“Yes, reverend,” Samiddhi replied. 

Then Ānanda and Samiddhi went up to the Buddha, bowed, sat down to one side, and told him what had happened. 

When they had spoken, the Buddha said to Ānanda, “I don’t recall even seeing the wanderer Potaliputta, Ānanda, so how could we have had such a discussion? The wanderer Potaliputta’s question should have been answered after analyzing it, but this futile man answered categorically.” 

When he said this, Venerable Udāyī said to him, “But perhaps, sir, Venerable Samiddhi spoke in reference to the statement: ‘Suffering includes whatever is felt.’”
